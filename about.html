Our Story

A century of tradition, hospitality, and the royal flavors of Bahawalpur.

Where We Began

In 1923, when Bahawalpur was a princely state of grand durbars and refined taste, this haveli was built for a family of courtiers in service to the Nawab's court. Its arches were carved by local artisans, its jali screens shaped to invite light and refuse heat. And at its heart stood a kitchen that never rested.

That kitchen belonged to the khansama, the head cook, whose recipes had traveled from royal kitchens into this home. Dishes that once graced a royal dastarkhwan were guarded here like heirlooms. They were passed from hand to hand, never written down, never altered.

Times changed. The princely state faded, the courts fell silent, and the haveli entered a quieter era. But the khansama's family stayed on as caretakers, keeping the courtyard swept, the brassware polished, and the recipes alive.

In the 1970s, they made a decision: the dastarkhwan should be laid again. This time for everyone. The doors opened, the carpets were spread, and the same recipes were served in the same brass. Today, the third and fourth generations run this kitchen the only way they know how: exactly as it always has been.

The Keepers of the Dastarkhwan

The recipes were never written down. They live in the hands that inherited them.

"A dastarkhwan is not fed by recipes alone. It is fed by those who remember."

Ustad Rahim Bakhsh, first khansama

Ustad Rahim Bakhsh never committed his dishes to paper. He believed a recipe could only be truly passed from one pair of hands to another: learned by watching, by stirring, by tasting at the elder's side. And so the kitchen's knowledge lives not in books, but in lineage.

Today, his great-grandson tends the same brass degs over the same slow fires. The spices are still ground each morning. The dough is still rested by feel, not by clock. What has been handed down is not merely a menu, but a discipline of care.

To keep the dastarkhwan is to keep a promise: that no guest leaves hungry, and no dish loses its soul. Every plate served today carries the memory of the hands that came before it.
